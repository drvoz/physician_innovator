In 20 minutes, teams of three or four turn a small synthetic dataset and a few resident voices into a research question, a labeled conceptual model and two hypotheses. The exercise rehearses Steps 1 to 5 of the [conceptual model tutorial](conceptual-model.html) in the Sheffield setting before students arrive on site.

> **All data and quotes below are synthetic.** They were invented for practice, describe no real Sheffield residents, and do not represent the clinic, Cornerstone Community Wellness or Royal Market. Students practice thinking skills only; no clinical advice is given or implied.

## Scenario

Sheffield, Illinois (about 800 residents) has one primary care physician at the OSF Sheffield Rural Health Clinic, a community wellness non-profit (Cornerstone Community Wellness) that runs fitness, food access and social support programs, and one grocery store with a commercial kitchen (Royal Market). Many residents with type 2 diabetes live on farms or in neighboring townships.

Your team has been handed a de-identified sample of 12 adults with type 2 diabetes and a page of short conversation notes. Your task is not to solve anything. It is to make your thinking visible: what question is worth asking, what concepts matter, and how you believe they connect.

## Synthetic conversation notes

- **Resident, 59, lives 18 miles out:** "I get to town when my neighbor drives. If I miss that, I miss the week."
- **Resident, 75, joined a wellness group last year:** "The cooking class showed me what to do with what I can actually afford. I feel like I know what I'm doing now."
- **Resident, 43, works two jobs:** "I know what I should eat. The problem is time and money, not knowing."
- **Program volunteer:** "The people who come every week mostly live close by. The ones I worry about don't come at all."
- **Store employee:** "People ask for easy, cheap meals. Fresh stuff sells when someone shows them how to use it."

## Run of show

| Time | Students do | Tutorial step | Product |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–2:00 | Form teams; facilitator reads the scenario and the synthetic-data note aloud | — | Teams ready |
| 2:00–5:00 | Scan the table and notes; write one research question with one outcome | Step 1 | Research question |
| 5:00–9:00 | Put each concept on a sticky note; label its role: exposure, outcome, mediator, moderator or confounder | Step 3 | Concept cards |
| 9:00–14:00 | Arrange cards into a model, left to right; draw arrows; mark + or − | Steps 4 and 5 | Draft model |
| 14:00–17:00 | Write two hypotheses; name one measure from the table for each concept used | Steps 5 and 6 | Hypotheses and measures |
| 17:00–20:00 | Two teams show their models; facilitator reveals the sample model; debrief | Step 7 | Shared insight |

## Mural board setup

Create one board per team with four labeled frames:

1. **Data:** paste the table and conversation notes.
2. **Concept cards:** blank sticky notes in five colors, one per role, with a color key.
3. **Model canvas:** a left-to-right area with "Outcome" pre-placed on the right.
4. **Hypotheses:** a template sticky: "Among [population], higher [A] will be associated with [higher / lower] [B]."

## Facilitator prompts

- At 4:00, if teams list several outcomes: "Which one would the community most want to change? Pick that one."
- At 8:00: "Which concept could explain why the program seems to help? That is your mediator candidate."
- At 11:00: "Who comes to the program, and who doesn't? Could the same thing affect both joining and HbA1c?"
- At 13:00: "Look at S07 and S11. Does the program seem to matter more for some people than others?"
- Throughout: "Point to the row or quote behind that arrow."

Participants have lower HbA1c and higher self-efficacy, but they also live much closer to town, so distance must be controlled for before crediting the program.

**Sample research question:** Among adults with type 2 diabetes in the Sheffield area, how does participation in community wellness programming relate to HbA1c?

**Sample hypotheses:**

- H1: Program participants will have lower HbA1c than non-participants, after accounting for distance and car access.
- H2: Higher self-efficacy will partly explain the link between participation and lower HbA1c.
- H3: The link between participation and lower HbA1c will be stronger among food-insecure residents.

**Measures from the table:** participation (In Cornerstone program), self-efficacy (1–10 score), HbA1c (%), food insecurity (Yes/No), distance (miles) and car access (Yes/No). Home cooking is a second mediator candidate worth discussing.

## Debrief questions (3 minutes)

1. Which arrow did your team argue about most, and what evidence settled it?
2. With only 12 synthetic people, what can this model *not* tell you? What would you need to observe or measure on site?
3. Distance and transport showed up in the data and the notes. Is that a confounder, a separate exposure, or the real need?
4. The resident who said "the problem is time and money, not knowing" is pushing back on a knowledge-based model. Which [theory](terms.html#common-theories-and-frameworks-in-health-research) fits her view better?
5. How would you check this model with the clinic, Cornerstone and Royal Market before treating it as your own?

## What this exercise models

- **Focusing a question** from messy, real-feeling information
- **Naming roles** for concepts rather than listing factors
- **Spotting confounding** (distance and transport sit behind both program participation and HbA1c)
- **Spotting moderation** (the program gap looks larger among food-insecure residents)
- **Humility about evidence:** a pattern in 12 rows is a hypothesis to test, not a finding
- **Community alignment:** the model is a draft to bring to partners, matching the Sheffield rotation's stance of offering ideas back rather than prescribing
