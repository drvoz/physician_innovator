## Overview

Work through seven steps to produce a one-page logic model that links your project's objectives to community priorities, names how it will be implemented, sets measurable targets and assigns every task to a partner. Each step ends with one completed template.

**A logic model** is your theory of change on one page: **if** we invest these inputs and carry out these activities, **then** we produce these outputs, which **lead to** short-term, intermediate and long-term outcomes.

The top row is what you control; the bottom row is the change you expect it to cause.

| Component | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | What resources will we invest? |
| Activities | What will we do? |
| Outputs | What will we produce or deliver, and how much? |
| Short-term outcomes | What changes first (knowledge, skills, processes)? |
| Intermediate outcomes | What changes in practice or results follow? |
| Long-term outcomes | What lasting health, equity or system impact do we expect? |
| Assumptions | What must be true for each link to hold? |
| External factors | What could affect results that we do not control? |

**The seven steps:**

1. Assemble the team and gather inputs
2. Define the problem and long-term goal
3. Align objectives with community priorities
4. Define implementation strategies and outcomes
5. Identify measurable indicators of success
6. Establish roles and responsibilities
7. Assemble, test and maintain the logic model

## Step 1: Assemble the team and gather inputs

**Goal:** the right people in the room and the evidence on the table before anyone drafts objectives.

1. Name a project lead and a facilitator (they should be different people).
2. Invite every partner who will deliver, fund, evaluate or be affected by the project, including community members with lived experience.
3. Collect existing evidence: community health needs assessments, local health department plans, partner reports, quality or claims data, and published research on the problem.
4. List the resources already committed or likely: funding, staff time, facilities, data access and partner contributions. These become your **inputs**.
5. Schedule the working sessions (one half-day session or three 90-minute sessions works well) and share the evidence a week ahead.

**Output:** a partner list, an evidence folder and a draft list of inputs.

## Step 2: Define the problem and long-term goal

**Goal:** one sentence everyone agrees describes the problem, and one sentence describing the change you ultimately want.

1. Write the problem statement: **who** is affected, **what** the gap is, **how big** it is, and **why** it persists.
2. Write the long-term goal as the change in health, equity or system performance you expect in three to five years.
3. Work backward: ask "what must change first for that to happen?" and note the answers. These become your intermediate and short-term outcomes in Step 4.
4. Test both sentences with community partners. If they would not describe the problem the same way, rewrite it with them.

**Template:**

- **Problem statement:** [Population] experiences [gap or condition] at [size or rate], compared with [benchmark], because of [key drivers].
- **Long-term goal:** By [year], [population] will have [improved condition], measured by [high-level measure].

**Output:** an agreed problem statement and long-term goal.

## Step 3: Align objectives with community priorities

**Goal:** every project objective answers a priority the community has already named.

1. List the community's top three to five priorities in the community's own words, with the source of each (needs assessment, listening session, advisory council, health department plan).
2. Draft three to five project objectives. Write each as a specific change you will test or deliver.
3. Map each objective to at least one priority in the matrix below. Cut or justify any objective that maps to none.
4. Note the organizational or funder interest each objective also serves, so the alignment is visible to all sides.
5. Review the matrix with community partners and record what they changed.

**Output:** a partner-reviewed alignment matrix.

## Step 4: Define implementation strategies and outcomes

**Goal:** a clear split between *what* you deliver and *how* you get it adopted, with outcomes defined at each level.

1. Name the **intervention**: the program, practice or service participants will receive.
2. Choose **implementation strategies**: the actions that get the intervention adopted and delivered well (training, role changes, reminders, audit and feedback, partner co-design). Use names from the ERIC compilation of 73 strategies where possible.
3. Specify each strategy: who does it, what they do, who it targets, when, how often, and why you expect it to work.
4. Define outcomes at three levels:
    - **Implementation:** acceptability, adoption, feasibility, fidelity, reach, cost, sustainability
    - **Service:** quality, timeliness, equity of the care or service delivered
    - **Participant or population:** health status, behavior, experience, cost
5. Sort the outcomes into short-term, intermediate and long-term, linking each back to the steps you worked backward through in Step 2.

## Step 5: Identify measurable indicators of success

**Goal:** each outcome has at least one indicator you can actually measure, with a baseline, a target and an owner.

1. For each outcome, write one or two indicators that are SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.
2. Define each indicator precisely: numerator, denominator and data source.
3. Record the baseline value, or the date by which you will establish it.
4. Set a target and the date it should be reached.
5. Check coverage with RE-AIM: include at least one indicator for **Reach**, **Effectiveness**, **Adoption**, **Implementation** and **Maintenance**.
6. Pair slow-moving outcome indicators with faster process indicators you can review monthly.
7. Stratify key indicators by population group so you can see whether gaps are closing.
8. Include at least one indicator chosen by community partners.

## Step 6: Establish roles and responsibilities

**Goal:** every activity and indicator has exactly one accountable partner, and the agreements behind those roles are written down.

1. List the major activities from Step 4 and the indicators from Step 5.
2. For each, assign RACI roles across partners:
    - **R, Responsible:** does the work
    - **A, Accountable:** owns the result; only one per row
    - **C, Consulted:** gives input before decisions
    - **I, Informed:** kept up to date
3. Fix any row with no A, or more than one.
4. Capture roles in a partnership agreement covering shared aims, each partner's commitments and budget (including payment for community partners' time), data sharing, decision-making, credit and authorship, and how results return to the community.
5. Set the governance rhythm: a steering group with community seats, a regular operations huddle and periodic community report-backs.

**Output:** a completed RACI, a partnership agreement and a meeting schedule.

## Step 7: Assemble, test and maintain the logic model

**Goal:** a one-page logic model that partners agree with and that guides implementation over the life of the project.

1. Transfer your work into the template below: inputs from Step 1, activities and outcomes from Step 4, outputs from your strategy table, and assumptions and external factors from your discussions.
2. Test the logic: read one thread aloud as "if... then... which leads to..." from an input to a long-term outcome. Wherever the link feels weak, add evidence, add an activity, or state it as an assumption.
3. Check that every outcome has an indicator (Step 5) and every activity has an accountable partner (Step 6).
4. Review the model with all partners and record sign-off.
5. Revisit it at each project milestone and update it as you learn.

**Final checklist:**

- [ ] Every objective maps to a named community priority
- [ ] Community partners reviewed the model and their changes are recorded
- [ ] Intervention and implementation strategies are listed separately
- [ ] Outputs (what you did) are distinct from outcomes (what changed)
- [ ] Every outcome has a SMART indicator with baseline, target, source and owner
- [ ] Each RE-AIM dimension has at least one indicator
- [ ] Every activity has exactly one accountable partner
- [ ] Assumptions and external factors are written down
- [ ] A date is set for the first review
